#### NEW:slide_typos_math_errors (Estimated Count: 150 comments)
Audience members frequently identify graphical and mathematical errors within the presentation slides. Specifically, viewers call out swapped strike prices in visual graphics (such as displaying 4350 and 4450 reversed on "Unrealized Loss" slides), incorrect calendar dates (such as displaying year 2025 dates for 2024 backtests), and math discrepancies in backtest tallies. A major recurring point of contention is why the presenter claims a \$2,000 net profit for a weekly credit spread backtest when a simple multiplication of the stated win/loss numbers (e.g., 26 wins at \$200 minus 14 losses at \$300) only yields \$1,000, forcing the presenter to explain average versus worst-case pricing in the comments section.

#### NEW:buy_and_hold_outperformance (Estimated Count: 140 comments)
A significant segment of the audience consistently points out that the options strategies demonstrated (especially the Wheel, covered calls, and synthetic long positions) often underperform a simple buy-and-hold campaign of the underlying ETF (like SPY or QQQ) over the identical time periods. Commenters emphasize that buying and holding requires zero management time, carries no options execution slippage, provides capital gains exposure, and is taxed at lower long-term capital gains tax rates compared to the highly taxed, short-term options premium cash flows.

### Questions Answered by SMB

1. **[#1.1 under "I always win if I keep rolling?"]**
   *Question*: Doesn't a covered call always win because the upfront cash premium collected is kept no matter what?
   *Answer*: Covered calls always provide cash, but the overall trade loses money if the stock's capital decline exceeds the premium received [13].
2. **[#45 under "Right Way to Trade Covered Calls"]**
   *Question*: If the stock drops in value more than the premium received, shouldn't you buy back the calls and sell the stock?
   *Answer*: The strategy is geared toward long-term investments where short-term paper declines are ignored while generating recurring cash flow [14].
3. **[#19.2 under "Simplest High Probability Technique"]**
   *Question*: Doesn't the mathematical model fall apart since one single loss wipes out nine previous credit spread wins?
   *Answer*: Professional traders run tight stop-losses on credit spreads so that the realized loss is much lower than the theoretical maximum risk [15].
4. **[#35 under "Options Strategies For Earnings"]**
   *Question*: What DTE and strikes are best for pre-earnings volatility run-ups?
   *Answer*: Target entry a few weeks out and slightly out of the money to avoid paying high at-the-money time premiums [12].
5. **[#36 under "Simplest High Probability Technique"]**
   *Question*: How far out in DTE do you have to look to find sufficient trading volume on a 10 Delta option?
   *Answer*: Volume is highly asset-dependent, but liquid index products like SPY have plenty of volume at a 10 Delta [16].

Instead of SPX or high-priced tech stocks, structure spreads around cheap, highly liquid ETFs or mid-cap equities so that beginners can trade defined-risk positions without risking their entire net asset value or violating broker capital requirements [7, 15, 26].

This tool must calculate the actual bid-ask slippage on multi-leg rolls and demonstrate exactly how one leg gains value faster than the other, teaching users how to actively manage and minimize a 10:1 risk-to-reward disaster instead of letting a spread run to max loss at expiration [2, 6, 42, 43].
3.  **An Integrated Expected Value (EV) & Volatility Smile Strike Scanner**: Build a scanner product that automatically filters the options chain for contracts displaying positive expected expectancy based on historical volatility (IV vs. RV) rather than purely relying on Delta [5, 8, 13, 21, 44].
