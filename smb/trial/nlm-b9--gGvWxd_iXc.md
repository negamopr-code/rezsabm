PART A — Handbook Chapter Content

### Setup
*   **Instrument**: Alphabet stock, formerly known as Google (Google/GOOG) [1].
*   **Structure**: Put spread (specifically, a long put at a higher strike price and a short put at a lower strike price, legged in sequentially) [2-4].
*   **Strikes/Deltas**: Long 1125 put [2, 3] and Short 1090 put (referred to as the "ten ninety put") [2, 3].
*   **DTE (Days to Expiration)**: Not explicitly stated in the transcript for this specific trade [1-3].
*   **Entry Trigger**: Unprecedented market volatility with wild, relatively predictable swings in both directions (specifically during the COVID-19 pandemic starting in late February 2020) [1]. The trader buys a long put (e.g., the 1125 put) on a day when the stock has a large rally. On a subsequent trading day when the stock experiences a "ferocious sell-off", the trader sells a lower-strike put (e.g., the 1090 put) on the same options chain [1, 2]. The entry is triggered and successful if the trader timing-wise can sell the short put for a larger premium than what was originally paid for the long put [2, 3].

### Management and Exit Rules
*   **Risk-Free Legging In**: Once the short put is sold for more premium than was paid for the long put, all risk is completely removed from the trade [2, 3].
*   **Expiration Resolution**: The trade is held to expiration to capture the full differential value [3, 4].
    *   *If the stock rallies or stays high*: Both options expire completely worthless, and the trader pockets the net cash credit collected upfront as net profit [3].
    *   *If the stock sells off*: The long put appreciates significantly more than the loss on the short put, yielding a large bonus profit on top of the initial cash credit [3].

### Stated Edge or Statistics
*   **Risk Mitigation**: Legging into the put credit spread at a net credit guarantees a profit with zero possibility of loss, while attaching a high-payoff "lottery ticket" if the market continues to sell off [3].
*   **Case Study Performance**: Buying the 1125 put for "thirty eight ninety two" and selling the 1090 put the next day for "forty dollars and sixty-two cents" (totaling "four thousand and sixty two dollars") yielded a positive cash flow of "one hundred seventy dollars" [2, 3]. At expiration, the 1125 put settled at "fifty to seventy" and the 1090 put settled at a negative "seventeen seventy," resulting in an ultimate total profit of "three thousand six hundred seventy dollars" [3].

### Caveats
*   **Timing dependency**: The strategy requires "a little bit of luck with timing" across volatile trading sessions to execute both legs at prices that yield a net credit [3, 5].
*   **Profit Cap**: Selling the lower strike short put caps the maximum potential gains that the long put option could have captured on its own [3].
